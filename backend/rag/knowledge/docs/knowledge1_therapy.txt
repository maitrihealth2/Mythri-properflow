Psychodynamic Therapy Assumption Description 
Assumption of psychological causation Problems brought to psychotherapy can be discussed in terms of thoughts and feelings. Mental disorders are conceptualised as organisations of conscious or unconscious beliefs, thoughts, and feelings. Assumption of limitations of consciousness and the influence of unconscious mental states 
Understanding conscious experiences requires referring to unaware mental states, often motivated by a desire for safety. Helping patients become aware of unconscious expectations can aid in gaining control of emotions and behaviour. 
Assumption of internal representations of interpersonal relationships 
Interpersonal relationships, especially attachment relationships, are central to personality organisation. Mental representations of these relationships form schematic mental structures that shape interpersonal expectations and self-representations. Assumption of ubiquity of psychological conflict Wishes, affects, and ideas can be in conflict, causing distress and potentially undermining the development of psychological capacities. 
Assumption of psychic defences Defences are mental operations that distort conscious mental states to reduce anxiety. Self-serving distortions of mental states are a common feature of human information processing. 
Assumption of complex meanings Behaviour can be understood in terms of underlying mental states that may not be explicit in action or awareness. Different psychodynamic orientations may identify different concealed meanings behind the same behaviours. 
Assumption of emphasis on the therapeutic relationship 
Establishing an attachment relationship with a clinician is helpful. Different therapeutic schools explain this differently, but converging theorisation and research data suggest that engagement with an understanding adult triggers human capacities for relatedness that are therapeutic. Assumption of the validity of a developmental perspective Psychodynamic psychotherapists are oriented to the developmental aspects of their patients’ problems and work to optimise developmental processes.

Psychodynamic Therapy Freudian psychoanalysis Sigmund Freud’s psychoanalytic theory, which emphasises the inﬂuence of childhood experiences on adult psychopathology, has been inﬂuential in the ﬁeld of psychology. While later psychoanalytic theories have built upon and sometimes diverged from Freud’s ideas, the developmental perspective remains a core aspect of psychodynamic psychotherapy. Despite criticisms of its emphasis on early childhood and the challenges of empirical validation, the developmental model continues to be a valuable framework for understanding psychological disturbance. STRUCTURAL OBJECT RELATIONS: OTTO KERNBERG Otto Kernberg’s structural object relations theory, a synthesis of the Klein-Bion model and modern structural theory, emphasises the role of affects in the motivational system. Kernberg’s approach to neurotic problems focuses on the patient’s current thinking and utilises psychiatric diagnostic categories to predict treatment response. His work on personality disorders, particularly borderline personality disorder, highlights the importance of ego structures and aggressive impulses, and his transference-focused psychotherapy has shown efﬁcacy in treating these disorders.
