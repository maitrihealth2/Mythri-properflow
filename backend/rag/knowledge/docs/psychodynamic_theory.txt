[PSYCHODYNAMIC_THEORY]

CORE ASSUMPTIONS OF PSYCHODYNAMIC THERAPY

1. Assumption of psychological causation
Problems brought to psychotherapy can be discussed in terms of thoughts and feelings. Mental disorders are conceptualised as organisations of conscious or unconscious beliefs, thoughts, and feelings.

2. Assumption of limitations of consciousness and the influence of unconscious mental states
Understanding conscious experiences requires referring to unaware mental states, often motivated by a desire for safety. Helping patients become aware of unconscious expectations can aid in gaining control of emotions and behaviour.

3. Assumption of internal representations of interpersonal relationships
Interpersonal relationships, especially attachment relationships, are central to personality organisation. Mental representations of these relationships form schematic mental structures that shape interpersonal expectations and self-representations.

4. Assumption of ubiquity of psychological conflict
Wishes, affects, and ideas can be in conflict, causing distress and potentially undermining the development of psychological capacities.

5. Assumption of psychic defences
Defences are mental operations that distort conscious mental states to reduce anxiety. Self-serving distortions of mental states are a common feature of human information processing.

6. Assumption of complex meanings
Behaviour can be understood in terms of underlying mental states that may not be explicit in action or awareness. Different psychodynamic orientations may identify different concealed meanings behind the same behaviours.

7. Assumption of emphasis on the therapeutic relationship
Establishing an attachment relationship with a clinician is helpful. Different therapeutic schools explain this differently, but converging theorisation and research data suggest that engagement with an understanding adult triggers human capacities for relatedness that are therapeutic.

8. Assumption of the validity of a developmental perspective
Psychodynamic psychotherapists are oriented to the developmental aspects of their patients' problems and work to optimise developmental processes.

FREUDIAN PSYCHOANALYSIS

Sigmund Freud's psychoanalytic theory emphasises the influence of childhood experiences on adult psychopathology and has been highly influential in psychology. Later psychoanalytic theories have built upon and sometimes diverged from Freud's ideas, but the developmental perspective remains a core aspect of psychodynamic psychotherapy. Despite criticisms of its emphasis on early childhood and the challenges of empirical validation, the developmental model continues to be a valuable framework for understanding psychological disturbance.

STRUCTURAL APPROACH

Key theorists and contributions:
- Heinz Hartmann: proposed the developmental assumption of change of function. Key concepts: secondary autonomy, the ego's undifferentiated origin.
- Erik Erikson: focused on the interaction of social norms and biological drives in generating self and identity. Key concepts: eight developmental stages, identity diffusion, basic trust vs. basic mistrust.
- René Spitz: ascribed primary importance to the mother-infant interaction. Key concepts: organisers, shifts in psychological organisation.
- Edith Jacobson: assumed a state of primitive fusion between early object representations and self-representation. Key concept: primitive fusion.
- Hans Loewald: proposed that integrative experience is at the centre of development. Key concepts: internalisation, relationality, interactional and intersubjective experiences.

The structural approach in psychoanalysis has been criticised for its quasi-physiological character and emphasis on sexuality, but has been reinterpreted and adapted over time. While some analysts remain committed to updated structural models, others have embraced object relations theory or developed alternative models like relational theory and self-psychology. Its influence persists in developmental theories and recent research linking anxiety, craving, and dreaming to instinctual drive states.

ANNA FREUD'S DEVELOPMENTAL MODEL

- Developmental Model: emphasises developmental progress through resolving conflicts (e.g., a child finding a compromise among wishes, needs, and perceptions).
- Role of Drives: maintains the Freudian view that drives are crucial in ego and superego formation.
- Influence of Child-Rearing: acknowledges the impact of child-rearing on ego and superego development.
- Developmental Lines: a metaphor for continuous and cumulative child development (e.g., dependency to emotional self-reliance, egocentrism to social partnerships).
- Pathology: defined as discrepancies or lags in developmental lines (arrest or regression along a line).
- Specific Issues: disturbances identified in narcissism, object relatedness, aggression control, etc.
- Anxiety: distinguished objective anxiety (fear of the external world) from internal anxiety (fear of impulses, wishes, feelings).
- Empirical Support: her work with infants at Hampstead war nurseries supported her theories, including a study on children's reactions to Scud missile attacks in Israel.

MARGARET MAHLER'S SUBPHASES OF SEPARATION-INDIVIDUATION

- Normal Autism (first weeks of life): a state of undifferentiation between infant and mother.
- Symbiotic Phase (around 2-4 months): infant experiences a sense of unity with the mother.
- Differentiation (around 4-9 months): infant begins to distinguish themselves from the mother.
- Practicing (9-15-18 months): infant explores their own autonomy and independence.
- Rapprochement (second half of the second year): infant seeks closeness and reunion with the mother.
- Consolidation of Individuality (beginning of the third year): infant solidifies their sense of self and individuality.

JOSEPH SANDLER

- Integration of Psychoanalysis: modernised psychoanalysis for integration with developmental sciences.
- Common Ground: found common ground between American ego psychology and British object relations theory.
- Representational World: introduced the concept of the representational world, similar to schema theory.
- Motivation: placed feeling states at the centre of motivation, rather than psychic energy.
- Background of Safety: introduced the concept where the ego aims to maximise safety.
- Drives: explained the influence of drives on behaviour through their impact on feelings.
- Patient-Therapist Relationships: suggested patients create relationships to actualize unconscious fantasies.
- Internal Object Representations: differentiated between past unconscious (hypothetical structures) and present unconscious (works irrationally, concerned with current experience).
- Developmental Model of Depression: proposed a model of depression based on the representational world construct.
- Projective Identification: explained projective identification without extravagant assumptions.
- Therapeutic Phenomena: his models explain phenomena like countertransference.
- Influence: his theories are widely used but not always attributed to him.

OBJECT RELATIONS THEORY

Two broad categories:
- Hard Object Relations Theories: derive relationships from drive theory, emphasising the individual's experience of being with others, including the therapist. Key figures: Melanie Klein, Ronald Fairbairn, Otto Kernberg. Example: Klein's work on the depressive position and paranoid-schizoid position.
- Soft Object Relations Theories: derive drives from object relations, focusing on the individual's experience of being with others, including the therapist. Key figures: Michael Balint, D. W. Winnicott, Heinz Kohut. Example: Bion's work on emotional development and pathology.

Key concepts and controversies:
- Projective Identification: an interpersonal process where the self gets rid of feelings by evoking the same feelings in another self. Some characterise it as a defensive mechanism.
- Infancy Roots of Projective Identification: the process is thought to originate in infancy, though there is debate over how complex infants' psychological capacities really are.
- Innate Violent Tendencies: Klein suggested children innately possess ruthless and sadistic fantasies — a controversial claim that violent tendencies are innate rather than environmentally caused.
- Mediation of Genetic Vulnerability: this model has informed understanding of how genetic vulnerability is mediated, supported by evidence of physical aggression and destructiveness in early childhood.
- Paranoid-Schizoid Position: a predominance of this position leads to mental disorder.
- Depressive Position: relative stability of this position reflects mental health, though Bion argued it is never permanently achieved.
- Psychotic States: intense annihilatory/persecutory anxieties lead to projective identification and reintrojection, and can produce delusions of external control over mind or body.
- Neurotic Problems: consequences of unresolved depressive anxiety.
- Depression: arises from the experience of loss, reminding the person of damage caused to the good object; chronic depression occurs when a person cannot escape the fear of injuring the loved object.
- Narcissistic Character Structure: a defence against envy and dependence, characterised by ruthless use of others and a professed lack of need for them.
- Evidence for Early Origins of Mental Disorders: evidence suggests most adult mental disorders are foreshadowed in infancy, though there is debate over interpreting psychoanalytic infant observation.
- Early Brain Development: increasingly seen as pivotal in the development of mental disorders.

HEINZ KOHUT AND SELF PSYCHOLOGY

Core developmental concepts:
- Narcissistic Development proceeds along a unique path, with parents serving as selfobjects.
- Empathic Responses allow the unfolding of exhibitionism and grandiosity from the mirroring selfobject.
- Frustration promotes the modulation of infantile omnipotence through the transmuting internalisation of the mirroring selfobject.
- Consolidation of the Nuclear Self occurs gradually through the transmuting internalisation of the mirroring selfobject.
- Idealisation of Selfobjects leads to the development of ideals through internalisation.

Key concepts and criticisms:
- Cohesive Self: the goal to be attained — a self that remains constant over time. Criticism: may overlook the natural evolution of the self.
- Enfeebled Self: results from inadequate emotional attunement from selfobjects, leading to defensive pleasure-seeking behaviours. Criticism: may be overly simplistic.
- Anxiety: primarily experienced as a defect or lack of continuity in the self. Criticism: may neglect other sources of anxiety.
- Deficiency-Based Theory: assumes a lack of facilitating experiences leads to a primary psychic deficit. Criticism: seen as parent-blaming and overemphasising environmental influences.
- Psychosis: characterised by a complete lack of a cohesive sense of self.
- Personality Disorders: an enfeebled self vulnerable to temporary fragmentation.
- Neurosis: associated with a robust self structure.
- Narcissistic Personality: developmental arrest at the grandiose exhibitionistic self stage due to inadequate mirroring from parents. Criticism: overly deterministic, neglects genetic influences.
- Borderline Personality: inability to psychologically hold on to soothing selfobjects.
- Drug Addiction: seen as a way to fill a missing gap in the psyche.
- Mirror Neurons: brain cells activated when observing a movement analogous to one's own; considered somewhat far-fetched as direct confirmation of self-psychological ideas.
- Genetic Influences: increasing evidence of genetically rooted personality traits challenges purely environmental accounts.
- Passive Genotype-Environment Correlation: a child's genetically rooted characteristics can shape the family environment.
- Child-to-Parent Effect: a child's genetically rooted characteristics can influence the family environment.
- Family Environment Moderating Genetic Influence: qualities of the family environment can influence how genetic predispositions are expressed.

STRUCTURAL OBJECT RELATIONS: OTTO KERNBERG

Otto Kernberg's structural object relations theory synthesises the Klein-Bion model with modern structural theory, emphasising the role of affects in the motivational system. His approach to neurotic problems focuses on the patient's current thinking and uses psychiatric diagnostic categories to predict treatment response. His work on personality disorders, particularly borderline personality disorder, highlights the importance of ego structures and aggressive impulses. His transference-focused psychotherapy has shown efficacy in treating these disorders.

THE INTERPERSONAL RELATIONAL APPROACH

Developmental building blocks:
- Nonreflective, presymbolic behaviour: organises relational fields around reciprocal influences and mutual regulation.
- Affective permeability: shared experience of intense affect across permeable boundaries (direct resonances, interpersonal dyads).
- Self-other configurations: organisation of experience into self-other configurations.
- Intersubjectivity: mutual recognition of self-reflective agents.

Related approaches and evidence:
- Interpersonal Therapy (IPT): strong evidence for depression in adults, some evidence for adolescents. Note: may have deviated from its psychodynamic roots.
- Psychoanalytic Adaptations: controlled studies show effectiveness for mood disorders and eating disorders; retain a focus on the relationship-seeking aspect of human character.

SCHEMA THEORY

- Boesky (1988), Peterfreund (1971), Rosenblatt and Thickstun (1977): integrated psychoanalysis and systems theory around unconscious relationship procedures; applied to psychotherapy based on describing unconscious processes.
- Horowitz et al. (1995): integrated British object relations theory, cognitive science, and systems theory around "person schemas"; applied in cognitive analytic therapy addressing underlying relationship patterns.
- Anthony Ryle: integrated psychoanalysis and systems theory around reciprocal role procedures (care, dependency, control, submission, love, anger); applied to therapist-patient correspondence in cognitive analytic therapy.
- Daniel Stern: integrated psychoanalysis and systems theory around self-development phases (emerging, core, subjective, narrative); applied to the development of personality disorders and neurosis.
- Stern (extended): described emergent moments, schematic representations, feeling shapes, and protonarrative envelopes; applied to distortions in basic schemas leading to psychopathology.

MENTALIZATION THEORY

Core processes and what disruption looks like:
- Labelling and understanding of affect: identifying and comprehending emotions. Disruption leads to difficulty in emotional regulation and expression.
- Arousal regulation: managing emotional arousal and reactivity. Disruption leads to difficulty in self-soothing and emotional control.
- Effortful control: regulating impulses and behaviour. Disruption leads to impulsivity and difficulty in goal-directed behaviour.
- Mentalizing/mind-reading capacities: understanding and inferring mental states of oneself and others. Disruption leads to difficulty in social cognition and empathy.

Areas of dysfunction and consequences:
- Agentive Self: dysfunctions are central to psychopathology, contributing to severe personality disorder or borderline personality organisation.
- Interpersonal Interpretive Function: dysfunctions lead to problems understanding others, contributing to disorganised attachment and disorganisation of the self.
- Attachment System: disorganised attachment results from incongruent mirroring and an insecure base, potentially producing "alien self" formation, where ideas/feelings are experienced as not belonging to the self.
- Trauma Impact: trauma exacerbates vulnerabilities in individuals with insecure early environments, producing inhibited playfulness, impaired affect regulation, attentional control difficulties, and potential failure of mentalization.
- Treatment Approach: aims for a stable, coherent representation of the patient's internal world; a randomised controlled trial showed promising results in a partial hospital setting.
